Know your wasabi

Real wasabi vs. the green paste

Wasabi is a rhizome from the same family as mustard and cabbage, traditionally grown in cool, shaded mountain streams in Japan. It’s slow and tricky to farm, which is why most of what we call “wasabi” is actually horseradish with mustard and green colouring.

Fresh wasabi is grated on a fine grater in small circles to release its aroma. The heat rises through the nose rather than burning the tongue, and fades within moments, leaving a clean, sweet, green flavour.

Fresh wasabi

Pale green, fragrant, gentle heat. Grate just before eating; it loses its punch in about 15 minutes.

Tube or powder

Bright green, sharper and longer-lasting. Usually horseradish-based. Check the label for wasabi content.

The humble onigiri

Rice balls: Japan’s favourite packed lunch

Onigiri are simply warm, lightly salted rice shaped by hand around a filling and often wrapped in a strip of nori. They travel well and taste great at room temperature, which makes them perfect for lunch boxes and picnics.

  • Wet your hands and rub them with a pinch of salt before shaping.
  • Press firmly enough to hold, but not so hard that the rice turns dense.
  • Popular fillings: umeboshi, flaked salted salmon, tuna with mayonnaise, or kombu simmered in soy.
  • Add the nori just before eating if you like it crisp.
Questions

Japanese home cooking, answered

What does “shun” mean in Japanese cooking?

Shun is the moment when an ingredient is at its seasonal best. Japanese home cooks plan meals around it, so a spring table looks and tastes very different from an autumn one. Our ingredient of the month follows this idea.

Is the wasabi in most tubes real wasabi?

Often not. Many tubes and powders are mostly horseradish and mustard coloured green, with little or no wasabi. Fresh wasabi rhizome is milder, sweeter and more aromatic. Check the ingredients list if you want the real thing.

Do I need a rice cooker to make Japanese rice?

No. A heavy pot with a tight lid works well. Rinse the rice, soak it, bring it to the boil, then cook on the lowest heat for about 13 minutes and rest it, lid on, for 10 minutes.

Can Japanese food be vegetarian or vegan?

Yes. Shojin ryori, the temple cooking tradition, is entirely plant-based. Use kombu and dried shiitake for dashi, and build meals around tofu, vegetables, seaweed and rice.

How long does homemade dashi keep?

Store it covered in the fridge and use it within three days, or freeze it in small portions for up to a month.

How we shop

Start at the vegetable stall

Japanese home cooking begins with what looks best that day. We shop for vegetables first, then plan the meal around them: a soup, a main, one or two small sides and rice. This “one soup, three dishes” rhythm keeps meals varied without being complicated.

You don’t need a specialist shop for most of our recipes. Soy sauce, rice vinegar, miso, kombu and short-grain rice cover a great deal of ground.
